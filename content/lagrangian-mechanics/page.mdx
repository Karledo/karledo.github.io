You're probably already quite familiar with one of the forms of classical mechanics, that being Newtonian mechanics. Newtonian mechanics is most likely the formulation of classical mechanics that you used all the way through secondary school, but let's first understand how we use Newtonian mechanics.

<AnchoredHeader id="building-from-newtonian-mechanics">Building from Newtonian Mechanics</AnchoredHeader>

Newtonian mechanics works on the basis of three laws.

<div className="space-y-16"><div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-y-2">
  <span className="text-foreground-200 text-sm">Newton's First Law</span>
  <span className="text-center w-[75%]">*A body remains at rest or in uniform motion in a straight line unless acted upon by a resultant force.*</span>
</div>

<div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-y-2">
  <span className="text-foreground-200 text-sm">Newton's Second Law</span>
  <span className="text-center w-[75%]">*The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the resultant force acting upon it and inversely proportional to its mass*</span>
</div>

<div className="flex flex-col items-center gap-y-2">
  <span className="text-foreground-200 text-sm">Newton's Third Law</span>
  <span className="text-center w-[75%]">*For every action there is a reaction equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, acting on two different bodies*</span>
</div></div>

So, in theory, if we know the initial conditions of a body, i.e. its starting position and velocity, as well as all the forces acting on the body we can perfectly predict its motion forever.

Forces can be represented by vectors, and for most questions, we can resolve these vectors very easily.

<AnchoredHeader id="simple-pendulum">Simple Pendulum</AnchoredHeader>

Imagine you had a particle attached to an inextensible string.

Defining <Latex text="$\theta$" /> as the angle of the string from downwards vertical, taken positive on the right, and negative on the left, right as the positive <span className="text-visual-red"><Latex text="$x$" /></span>, and downwards being the positive <span className="text-visual-green"><Latex text="$y$" /></span>. We have one equation of motion for each component, <Latex text="$x$" /> and <Latex text="$y$" />.

<FixedPendulum />

<Latex text="$$
\begin{aligned}
-T\sin(\theta) &= m\ddot{x} \\
mg - T\cos(\theta) &= m\ddot{y}
\end{aligned}
$$" />

We also have the restriction that the string is inextensible, so we can also state that

<Latex text="$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= L\sin(\theta) \\
y &= L\cos(\theta)
\end{aligned}
$$" />

And then we can also differentiate with respect to time, and remembering to use the chain rule, we get these results

<Latex text="$$
\begin{gathered}
\dot{x} = L\dot{\theta}\cos\theta \\
\ddot{x} = L\ddot{\theta}\cos\theta - L\dot{\theta}^2\sin\theta \\
\dot{y} = -L\dot{\theta}\sin\theta \\
\ddot{y} = -L\ddot{\theta}\sin\theta - L\dot{\theta}^2\cos\theta
\end{gathered}
$$" />

I'm not going to bore you with the algebra to eliminate <Latex text="$\ddot{x}$" />, <Latex text="$\ddot{y}$" /> and <Latex text="$T$" />, but we get to a nice equation for the *angular acceleration*, which ends up being

<Latex text="$$\ddot{\theta} = -\frac{g}{L}\sin(\theta)$$" />

For our simple system here, it's very easy to resolve the forces to uncover the equations of motion for this system. But, as you will see later on, it becomes vastly more difficult to work with forces for more complex systems.

<AnchoredHeader id="more-complex-systems">Complex Systems</AnchoredHeader>

Currently, we've been working with just a single pendulum, and the equations of motion that we obtained were quite simple. But imagine that we wanted to have the same system, but with a double pendulum instead.

Let's start with the same definitions as before, but we will define a new angle <Latex text="$\theta_2$" />, which is the angle between the second string and the downwards vertical, taken positive on the right, and negative on the left.

<FixedDoublePendulum />

The equations of motion are now coupled. The first bob feels the tension in the second string as well as the tension in the first, and the second bob hangs from the first, so its motion includes the first bob’s. Each bob’s acceleration depends on the other’s, so neither pendulum can be solved without the other.

This is fine, and it's probably an obvious result that the two pendulums are interconnected. But the bigger problem is the algebra. We have to resolve both the *tensions* into Cartesian components, using the angles of each pendulum, and then eliminate <Latex text="$T_1$" />, and now <Latex text="$T_2$" />, alongside rewriting <Latex text="$\ddot{x}_1$" />, <Latex text="$\ddot{y}_1$" />, <Latex text="$\ddot{x}_2$" /> and <Latex text="$\ddot{y}_2$" /> in terms of <Latex text="$\theta_1$" /> and <Latex text="$\theta_2$" />.

That's a lot of work, and it's tedious and error-prone. We have to work with two tensions that we don't care about, just to get to the two angles we actually care about. On top of that, forces lend themselves to a Cartesian coordinate system, which doesn't fit the geometry of the problem nicely, when really, working in the two angles we defined is much more useful.

<AnchoredHeader id="the-lagrangian">The Lagrangian</AnchoredHeader>

This is where Lagrangian mechanics comes in, just as Newtonian mechanics is one formulation of classical mechanics we can use, Lagrangian mechanics is another, and it centres around the path of stationary action.

Action as a quantity is equal to *energy* <Latex text="$\times$" /> *time*, and for every possible path the bobs could take, we can evaluate its action.

We do that by defining the *Lagrangian*

<Latex text="$$L = T - V$$" />

where <Latex text="$T$" /> is the *kinetic energy* of the system and <Latex text="$V$" /> is the *potential energy* of the system. This equation was found by working backwards to find an equation that reproduces Newton's equations.

The action of the path is then given by

<Latex text="$$S = \int_0^{t_f} L\,dt$$" />

where <Latex text="$t_f$" /> is the end time.

How do we use the action to find the equations of motion? Out of all the possible paths that the bobs could take, they take the path for which the action is stationary. But first, we need to answer what it means for action to be stationary.

<AnchoredHeader id="stationary-points">Stationary Points</AnchoredHeader>

Now, imagine you had a function <Latex text="$f(x)$" />, and just so we can visualise what's going on, we will define it as some cubic

<Latex text="$$f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x$$" />

If we make a small change, <Latex text="$\epsilon$" />, where <Latex text="$\epsilon$" /> is sufficiently small, to <Latex text="$x$" /> then the change in the function is

<Latex text="$$
f(x + \epsilon) - f(x) = (3x^2 - 12x + 9)\,\epsilon + (3x - 6)\,\epsilon^2 + \epsilon^3
$$" />

The definition of a stationary point (a minimum, a maximum, or a stationary point of inflection) is where *gradient* equals zero. Equivalently, it is where the change in the function is zero, to *first order*.

This means a change in the function from a small nudge <Latex text="$\epsilon$" /> is only the result of orders of <Latex text="$\epsilon$" /> higher than first order, i.e. <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" />, <Latex text="$\epsilon^3$" />, <Latex text="$\epsilon^4$" /> and so on. But why does a zero first-order change mean we have a stationary point at that <Latex text="$x$" />?

Here, we need to recognise something about this result. A small shift to the right is <Latex text="$\epsilon > 0$" />, and a small shift to the left is <Latex text="$\epsilon < 0$"/>.

That means, at a given <Latex text="$x$" />, if the change in the function were dominated by <Latex text="$\epsilon$" />, then we would increase on one side, but decrease on the other, and we would no longer have a stationary point at <Latex text="$x$" />.

This is because there would be a point lower than the local minimum nearby, or a point greater than the local maximum nearby, depending on the nature of the stationary point.

But when the change is dominated by the <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" /> term, its sign on both sides is the sign of the <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" /> coefficient, so the change increases on both sides or decreases on both sides. If that coefficient is zero, the <Latex text="$\epsilon^3$" /> term dominates instead.

<StationaryGraph />

Since <Latex text="$\epsilon$" /> is sufficiently small, the higher the order, <Latex text="$n$" />, of <Latex text="$\epsilon$" />, i.e. <Latex text="$\epsilon$" />, <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" />, <Latex text="$\epsilon^3$" />, <Latex text="$\epsilon^4$" />, and so on, the smaller the value of <Latex text="$\epsilon^n$" /> gets. This means that the first-order term in <Latex text="$\epsilon$" /> will dominate the value of the change, followed by the <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" /> term and so on.

Therefore, once the first-order term vanishes, the <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" /> term decides the shape. If its coefficient is positive or negative, the change has the same sign on both sides, giving a minimum or maximum. If that coefficient is also zero, the <Latex text="$\epsilon^3$" /> term takes over, and the change flips sign across the point, giving a stationary point of inflection.

So we require the change to be zero, to first order:

<Latex text="$$
\begin{gathered}
(3x^2 - 12x + 9)\,\epsilon = 0 \\[0.5em]
3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 0 \\[0.5em]
3(x-1)(x-3) = 0
\end{gathered}
$$" />

This gives us <Latex text="$x = 3$"/> or <Latex text="$x = 1$" /> as our stationary points. This is exactly what we'd get from differentiating. In fact, essentially what we're doing is differentiating from first principles, but omitting the part where we take the limit.

<Latex text="$$
\begin{gathered}
f(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 9x\\[0.5em]
f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9 \\[0.5em]
3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 0 \\[0.5em]
3(x-1)(x-3) = 0
\end{gathered}
$$" />

We can determine the nature of each stationary point from the coefficient of <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" />. If it is positive, the change increases on both sides; if negative, it decreases on both sides. If it is zero, we look at the <Latex text="$\epsilon^3$" /> term: a change that increases on one side and decreases on the other indicates a point of inflection.

At <Latex text="$x = 3$"/>, the coefficient of <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" /> is <Latex text="$3(3) - 6 = 3$" />, so we're increasing on both sides, which means it's a *minimum*.

At <Latex text="$x = 1$"/>, the coefficient of <Latex text="$\epsilon^2$" /> is <Latex text="$3(1) - 6 = -3$" />, so we're decreasing on both sides, which means it's a *maximum*.

Note that our cubic has no stationary points of inflection.

So now we can make a rule.

Given <Latex text="$f(x)$" />, with <Latex text="$x$" /> unknown, find the change in the function <Latex text="$f(x + \epsilon) - f(x)$" />, take the first-order part <Latex text="$f'(x)\epsilon$" />, set it to zero, and solve for <Latex text="$x$" />.

In a similar way, we can do it for the action.

Imagine we were throwing a ball up and it fell back down, with up as the positive direction. I'm going to call the height of the ball at time <Latex text="$t$" />, <Latex text="$y(t)$" />, and I want to know the path that ball takes, to get to its height at time <Latex text="$t_f$" />, <Latex text="$y(t_f)$" />.

If we were at the path of stationary action, so the first-order part of the change in action is zero, then a tiny nudge <Latex text="$\eta(t)$" /> to this path, would change the action by <Latex text="$S[y + \eta] - S[y]$" />. We require that <Latex text="$\eta = 0$" /> at the start and end points, so we have the same start and destination for every path.

<StationaryAction />

Remember, <Latex text="$\eta$" /> is a general term for deviations in the path. We use it to keep track of the first-order changes. Just as in the function example, we end up with <Latex text="$f'(x)$" />, and <Latex text="$\epsilon$" /> drops out because only the coefficient matters.

Therefore, given the action <Latex text="$S[y]= \int_0^{t_f} L\,dt$"/>, where <Latex text="$L$" /> is the Lagrangian for the system, and the path <Latex text="$y(t)$" /> is unknown, find the change in the action <Latex text="$S[y + \eta] - S[y]$"/>, take the part of the action which is first-order in <Latex text="$\eta$" />, meaning the part linear in the nudge, so doubling <Latex text="$\eta$"/> doubles the action contributed by that part, set it to zero for every <Latex text="$\eta$" />, and solve for <Latex text="$y(t)$" />.

So for any system, if we can find the Lagrangian, we can determine which path has stationary action, and derive the equations of motion.

<AnchoredHeader id="the-euler-lagrange-equation">The Euler-Lagrange equation</AnchoredHeader>

Preferably, we don't want to work through first principles every time we want to use Lagrangian mechanics to answer a question, but conveniently, Euler, as usual, has generalised it for a general <Latex text="$L$" />.

For any system you can write down <Latex text="$L$" />, then write the Euler-Lagrange equation for each coordinate <Latex text="$q$" />, and then solve for the equation of motion for that coordinate.

<Latex text="$$\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}}\right) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}$$" />

In our ball example, the coordinate is just <Latex text="$y$" /> and the answer that we get is <Latex text="$\ddot{y} = -g$" />, which makes sense: the ball is just accelerating under gravity, and since y points up, we say that is the negative direction (unlike the pendulum).

We can now do the same to our double pendulum problem, for both <Latex text="$\theta_1$" /> and <Latex text="$\theta_2$" />, to figure out <Latex text="$\ddot{\theta}_1$" /> and <Latex text="$\ddot{\theta}_2$" />
